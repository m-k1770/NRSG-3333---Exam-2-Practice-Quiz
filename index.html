Foundations

Respiratory Foundations

Mechanics of breathing, gas exchange & perfusion, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, and ventilator modes.

Mechanics & Control of Breathing inhalation / exhalation

Inhalation: the diaphragm contracts/flattens and intercostals lift the ribs, expanding the thoracic cavity and dropping intrathoracic pressure below atmospheric — air rushes in. Exhalation: the diaphragm relaxes, the cavity recoils, pressure rises, and air leaves.

Why this matters clinically This negative-pressure principle is exactly why an open chest wound or a disconnected chest tube is dangerous — the vacuum is lost and the lung can collapse.

What actually drives your breathing?

Central chemoreceptors (brainstem) and peripheral chemoreceptors (aortic arch/carotid bodies) respond mainly to CO₂ and pH, not low oxygen. Rising CO₂ (hypercapnia) is the main stimulus to breathe faster/deeper.

Gas Exchange, Perfusion & Pulmonary Embolism

Ventilation moves air; perfusion moves blood. A V/Q mismatch (a mismatch between the two) is the root cause of hypoxia in conditions like pulmonary embolism and pulmonary edema.

PE signs/symptoms

  • Sudden dyspnea
  • Sudden anxiety / sense of "impending doom"
  • Pleuritic chest pain
  • Tachycardia
  • Hemoptysis
  • Diaphoresis, lightheadedness
  • Leg swelling/pain if DVT present

PE diagnostics & treatment

  • CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) — gold standard
  • D-dimer — screens for clot
  • Ultrasound — checks legs for DVT
  • V/Q scan — compares air movement to blood flow

Treatment: anticoagulants, thrombolytics, IVC filter, oxygen, pain control.

Pneumothorax Types

  • Simple/spontaneous: air enters the pleural space through a ruptured bleb; no trauma.
  • Traumatic: from blunt/penetrating chest injury or an iatrogenic puncture (thoracentesis, subclavian line).
  • Open ("sucking chest wound"): a chest wall defect lets air move freely in/out with each breath — produces a sucking sound and mediastinal flutter.
  • Tension pneumothorax: one-way valve effect — air enters but can't escape, pressure builds, and the mediastinum shifts away from the affected side, compressing the heart/great vessels.
Life-threatening Tension pneumothorax red flags: tracheal deviation away from the affected side, absent breath sounds on that side, hyperresonance to percussion, hypotension, JVD, severe respiratory distress. Needle decompression (large-bore needle, 2nd ICS midclavicular line) is the emergency bridge to chest tube placement.

Pleural Effusion & Assessment Findings

Abnormal fluid collection between the visceral and parietal pleura (normal is only 5–15 mL of lubricating fluid).

Transudate vs. exudate

  • Transudate — clear fluid, usually from heart failure (pressure/oncotic imbalance; pleura not diseased)
  • Exudate — from inflammation/infection/malignancy (bronchogenic carcinoma is the most common malignant cause)

Confirmed by chest X-ray/CT or thoracentesis, which also relieves dyspnea and provides fluid for analysis.

Chest Tubes & Drainage Systems

Restoring negative intrathoracic pressure — system types, setup, ongoing assessment, troubleshooting, removal, and documentation.

Chest Drainage Systems & the 3 Chambers

Chest tubes drain air, blood, or fluid from the pleural space (or mediastinum) and restore the negative pressure that keeps the lung inflated. Indications: pneumothorax, hemothorax, pleural effusion, post-thoracic surgery.

SystemHow it worksNotes
Traditional water seal (wet suction)3 chambers: collection, water seal, wet suction controlIntermittent bubbling = normal; needs sterile fluid in 2 chambers
Dry suction water sealCollection + water-seal chamber + dry suction regulator dialMost common (e.g., Atrium); quieter; indicator shows adequate suction
Dry suction one-way valveMechanical one-way valve, no water sealWorks even if knocked over; good for ambulatory patients
  • Collection chamber: where fluid drainage accumulates; mark date/time and level each shift.
  • Water-seal chamber: filled to the 2-cm line; one-way valve so air/fluid can leave the chest but can't re-enter. Tidaling confirms the system is connected and functioning.
  • Suction control: dial (dry) or water column (wet) that limits negative pressure — typically −20 cm H₂O unless ordered otherwise.
Remember Intermittent bubbling in the water seal is normal (especially early in a pneumothorax); continuous bubbling means an air leak somewhere in the system or at the insertion site.

Setup & Bedside Readiness

Initial bedside setup

  1. Hand hygiene; verify the order (suction vs. water seal only)
  2. Fill water-seal chamber to the 2-cm line with sterile water
  3. Connect patient tubing; keep the unit below chest level
  4. Connect wall suction, set device dial, increase wall suction until indicator shows adequate suction
  5. Confirm no dependent loops/kinks; apply occlusive dressing; assess for tidaling

Always at the bedside

Two rubber-tipped clamps, petroleum/Vaseline gauze, sterile 4x4 dressings, occlusive dressing/waterproof tape, a small container of sterile water or saline. If ambulating, these travel with the patient in a bag on the IV pole.

Ongoing Assessment (Q-shift & PRN)

  • Respiratory status: RR, work of breathing, SpO₂, lung sounds, pain/splinting
  • Insertion site/dressing: clean, dry, occlusive; check for subcutaneous emphysema (crepitus)
  • Water seal at the 2-cm mark, system upright and below chest
  • Tidaling present? Air leak (none/intermittent/continuous)?
  • Drainage color/character/amount — mark and total; report bright-red surges or rates >150–200 mL/hr (adult)
  • Tubing free of kinks, no dependent loops, no milking/stripping unless ordered
Never "Strip" or "milk" the tubing without an order — it creates dangerously high pressure that can cause a tension pneumothorax.

Assisting with Insertion & Removal

Nursing role during insertion

  • Obtain/analyze vitals and SpO₂; focused respiratory assessment
  • Verify allergies and review meds raising bleeding risk (NSAIDs, warfarin, aspirin) plus labs (H&H, INR)
  • After insertion: confirm placement by CXR, assess breath sounds/dyspnea, ensure dressing intact
Trauma alertNotify the provider if initial chest tube output is >1500 mL at once or >200 mL/hr — may indicate a vascular injury needing surgery.

Removal readiness & steps

Improved respiratory status, symmetric chest rise/fall, bilateral breath sounds, decreased drainage, no air leak, improved CXR.

  1. Premedicate ~30–60 min prior; position semi/high-Fowler's
  2. Patient takes a deep breath and holds it (or Valsalva) during removal
  3. Sterile occlusive dressing applied immediately, taped airtight on all sides

Documentation

Each shift, chart: tube type/location and system type; suction setting vs. device indicator; water-seal level and system position; full respiratory assessment; tidaling and air-leak status; drainage color/character/amount and running total; site/dressing condition and crepitus status; mobility/safety measures; education; provider notifications.

Sample chart entry "24F R lateral CT to disposable dry-suction water-seal drain. Order −20 cmH₂O; dial set, bellows at indicator. Water seal 2 cm; unit upright/below chest. Tidaling present; no air leak noted. Drainage serosanguineous 75 mL/8 hr; total 450 mL. Site CDI; no crepitus."

Central Venous Access Devices

Types of CVADs, insertion & placement verification, lumens, securement & dressings, complications, ongoing assessment, and blood sampling technique.

Why Central Access Is Needed

CVLs are often placed for emergency venous access in clients requiring fluid resuscitation and hemodynamic monitoring. Many clients who require CVADs are older adults, very young children, or those with chronic health conditions. Common indications include delivery of medication, fluids, and nutrition — especially for clients requiring long-term therapy or infusion of high-osmolarity solutions and vesicant medications that would damage a peripheral vein.

High-osmolarity examples (>900 mOsm/L)

  • TPN — high glucose concentration (often >10% dextrose) and electrolytes
  • Dextrose 10% — hypertonic compared to plasma
  • Mannitol 20% — osmotic diuretic
  • 3% Sodium Chloride (hypertonic saline) — used for hyponatremia, very irritating to veins
  • Chemotherapeutic agents (doxorubicin, vincristine, vinblastine, mitomycin)

Non-chemotherapy vesicants

  • Concentrated potassium chloride
  • Calcium chloride
  • Diazepam
  • Phenytoin (IV push)
  • Dopamine
  • Norepinephrine
Before use The insertion of any CVAD is an invasive medical procedure requiring informed consent from the client. All CVADs require radiologic confirmation of placement prior to use — by fluoroscopy during insertion of a tunneled line, or by X-ray after placement of a non-tunneled line/PICC. Catheter tip location must be documented in the client's medical record.

Non-Tunneled vs. Tunneled Central Venous Catheters

Non-Tunneled CVC

  • Short-term access — days to weeks
  • Inserted directly into a central vein: internal jugular, subclavian, or femoral
  • Higher infection risk — the insertion site is close to the vein entry
  • Commonly used in ICU or emergency settings
  • No cuff to help anchor or reduce infection

Tunneled CVC (Hickman, Broviac, Groshong)

  • Long-term access — weeks to months, or permanent
  • Inserted through a subcutaneous tunnel before reaching the vein
  • Lower infection risk — bacteria must travel farther to reach the bloodstream
  • Has a Dacron cuff that promotes tissue ingrowth, anchoring the catheter and providing a barrier
  • Uses: dialysis, chemotherapy, TPN, or long-term antibiotic therapy
  • Surgically placed (OR) or by interventional radiology (IR) using fluoroscopy; placement verification is provided by the surgeon or radiologist
Why it matters for nurses Tunneled vs. non-tunneled status impacts how the catheter is cared for (dressing changes, flushing protocols, infection monitoring), influences the choice of site and securement device, helps determine when the catheter should be removed, and changes patient education — tunneled CVCs need teaching for home care and signs of infection.

PICC Lines, Implanted Ports & Midlines

PICC (peripherally inserted central catheter)

  • Insertion site: basilic, cephalic, or brachial vein; length ~18–24 inches
  • Tip location: ends in the superior vena cava — central circulation
  • Use duration: long-term, up to 6 months
  • Often placed at the bedside with ultrasound guidance
  • X-ray required prior to use — confirms tip placement and rules out pneumothorax
  • Uses: chemotherapy, TPN, long-term antibiotics, frequent blood draws/medications
  • Avoid in clients with ESRD who need vein preservation for a future fistula/graft, or with a history of thrombosis/hypercoagulability

Implanted port (Infusaport)

  • Reservoir surgically placed under the skin, usually upper chest; catheter tip rests in the SVC
  • Requires a non-coring (Huber) needle, using sterile technique
  • Transparent dressing applied after access; changed per policy (often every 7 days if accessed)
  • Power-injectable ports can be identified by 3 tiny raised bumps in a triangle pattern — safe for high-pressure (power) CT/MRI contrast injections
  • Ports come in single or dual configurations; ALL ports require a non-coring needle

Midline catheter (peripheral — NOT central)

Length ~3–8 inches; same insertion site as a PICC, but the tip terminates in the upper arm, not beyond the axilla — it does not enter central circulation. Placed at the bedside with ultrasound. Use duration: intermediate, 1–4 weeks. Used for IV fluids, antibiotics, and pain medications. Not for vesicants, TPN, or agents needing central dilution. No X-ray required; lower CLABSI risk than a PICC. A midline is chosen when peripheral IV access is difficult or frequent labs are needed, but central access is not required.

When drawing blood from a line with more than one lumen, stop infusions into the other lumens first to prevent contamination or dilution of the sample.

Securement devices

Every CVAD must be stabilized and secured to the client. Dislodgement and premature removal increase complications such as infection, vessel injury, and treatment delays. Depending on location and device type, a CVAD may be stabilized with sutures or a sutureless device such as a stat-lock.

Dressing Care

A CVAD dressing should always look clean, dry, and occlusive. Maintaining an intact, occlusive dressing is essential to prevent CLABSI. If the dressing becomes loose, wet, or compromised in any way, it must be changed immediately.

Bio-patch rule Dressings with an antimicrobial bio-patch are valid for 7 days. If no patch is present, the dressing must be changed within 3 days and a patch applied.

Applying the Nursing Process & Ongoing Assessment

Nurses provide routine care and maintenance of CVADs after insertion, including ongoing assessment of the insertion site/infusion system, dressing changes with catheter stabilization, accessing/de-accessing, flushing and locking (saline and heparin), and blood sampling. The nurse also assesses the ongoing need for a CVL per institutional policy (usually every 24 hours), documents its necessity, and advocates for removal as soon as it is no longer indicated.

Always heparin lock A port must always be heparin locked prior to de-accessing.

Blood Sampling from a CVAD

Blood sampling from a CVAD is the responsibility of the registered nurse. Its main advantage is reducing pain and anxiety by avoiding repeated peripheral venipunctures, but it carries risk of infection, occlusion, and inaccurate labs if done improperly.

StepRationale
Stop infusions into the catheter's lumensPrevents substances from interfering with the blood sample
Choose the appropriate lumen (largest, or farthest from the heart if staggered exits)Blood draw requires a large lumen for accurate sampling
Scrub the needleless connector ≥15 seconds; let dry completelyPrevents microorganism and substance contamination
Attach a prefilled 10-mL syringe of preservative-free NS; flush 10–20 mL; aspirate slowly for bloodA 10-mL syringe generates lower pressure, preventing lumen rupture/occlusion
Clear the dead space: discard method (waste 2–25 mL) or push-pull method (aspirate/reinfuse 4–6 mL x4 cycles)Removes blood diluted by flush solution; push-pull reduces phlebotomy-associated blood loss
Flush thoroughly with 10–20 mL preservative-free NS afterwardPrevents occlusion
Never Use a CVAD infusing parenteral nutrition for blood sampling — this increases CLABSI risk. If signs of an air embolism occur during the procedure, place the client on the left side in Trendelenburg or left lateral decubitus position, call the rapid response team, and notify the provider.

Tracheostomy Care Essentials

Routine care

  • Clean/replace inner cannula q8–12h with sterile technique
  • Clean stoma and change dressing daily/when soiled, circular motion outward
  • Always suction before cleaning the site
  • Never cut regular gauze — use pre-cut trach gauze only

Bedside emergency equipment

  • Obturator from the current tube
  • Suction equipment, oxygen source
  • Manual ventilation (bag-valve-mask)
  • Spare trach tube: same size and one size smaller

Applying Tanner's Model: Recognizing Airway Emergencies

Tanner's Model (Noticing → Interpreting → Responding → Reflecting) framework applied to airway case studies:

  • Aspiration risk: a patient lying flat drinking thin liquids despite precautions who suddenly coughs and desaturates — reposition upright, encourage coughing, get help immediately.
  • NPA monitoring: increasing blood at the nares plus noisy breathing and falling SpO2 suggests secretions/clots partially obstructing the airway — report immediately even if unsure.
  • Tracheostomy site needing care: redness, crusted secretions, and gurgling breath sounds indicate the site needs suctioning first, then cleaning.
Exam takeaway As a student, "noticing" something is off and reporting it — even without knowing the exact diagnosis — is correct clinical judgment.

Wound Care & Pressure Injuries

Wound classification, healing intention, pressure injury staging, debridement, dressings, and surgical drains.

Wound Classification & Healing Intention

By cause

  • Surgical: intentional, controlled, sterile — primary intention
  • Traumatic: unplanned, often contaminated — secondary intention
  • Neuropathic/vascular: impaired sensation or circulation
  • Pressure: ischemia from unrelieved pressure over a bony prominence

Healing by intention

  • Primary: edges approximated (sutures/staples), minimal tissue loss, fast healing
  • Secondary: edges cannot be approximated, heals from base up via granulation
  • Tertiary: delayed primary closure — left open, then surgically closed once clean
Test trap Surgical/acute wounds with approximated edges → sterile technique. Pressure/chronic/open wounds → clean technique focused on moisture balance and infection prevention.

Postoperative Mobility Considerations

Early ambulation reduces atelectasis, hypostatic pneumonia, GI discomfort, and thromboembolic events. When getting a patient up for the first time, watch for orthostatic hypotension (a drop of ≥20 mmHg systolic or ≥10 mmHg diastolic from supine to standing, with dizziness/weakness/fainting) — assess BP supine, sitting, then 2–3 minutes after standing.

Never forget A fully dislodged CVAD requires direct pressure and a rapid response call — there is a risk of air embolus at the exit site.

"Call the Provider Now" Checklist (across all devices)

  • Sudden increase in drainage (chest tube >150–200 mL/hr; CVAD site bleeding)
  • New or worsening respiratory distress, tracheal deviation, hypoxia
  • Rapidly expanding subcutaneous emphysema
  • Persistent, unexplained continuous air leak
  • Device failure or accidental removal of any tube/line
  • Signs of infection: fever >100.4°F, redness/purulent drainage, rising WBC
